# 08 — Narrative craft

The rendering is what people notice. The structure is why it works. These observations come from the reference deck's beat lists, on-screen text and speaker notes.

## The arc

| Act | Scenes | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Cold open | A deliberately dull white template; the deck then edits itself on screen | Set expectations low, then break them. Establish the motif. |
| Evidence | The real chart; the anchor; the loop | Three claims, each as one physical metaphor |
| Scale | A country of agents; thirty steps; a year in a day; how far you can see; select all | Each scene ends at a larger scale than it began |
| Stakes | Aim; two futures | The same power, one small difference |
| Return | The whole talk on one curve; "you are here"; the dull template again | Recap by showing, then hand the question to the audience |

The ending is a **false ending**: the white "Any questions?" slide returns, and the note says to let people think it is over. Then the deck types its own question, selects the whole slide, and tilts it by exactly one degree, paying off a number established three scenes earlier. It ends on the cheapest possible frame.

## One scene, one shape

Every scene follows the same three moves:

1. **An establishing image** you can read in a second (a paper boat on a calm sea).
2. **One transformation** that *is* the argument (the sea rises; the chain does not get longer).
3. **A scale break or decisive act** (the chain link is deleted; the boat sails).

And every scene ends by handing its last object to the next transition.

## Make the metaphor literal

The strongest scenes do not illustrate a claim; they build an object whose geometry is the claim.

| Claim | Object |
|---|---|
| A linear chart hides exponential growth | The chart is fan-fold paper that has to physically unfold upward, through the clouds |
| Intuition is anchored to today | A boat on a chain of fixed length while the sea rises |
| 26% of the work | A fan with 26 blades next to a single sliver |
| Twice as fast | A 24-tooth gear driving a 12-tooth gear |
| 1.5× per step, thirty times | Dominoes, each 1.5× the last, from a desk to a mountain |
| Days are not equal | A calendar whose cells resize by how much happens in each |
| One degree off | An arrow's line followed from a room to the nearest star |
| Same power, different aim | One landscape, two versions, a draggable line between them |

Say in the notes what the picture encodes, so the speaker can point at it.

## A motif that acts

The reference deck has one recurring UI element: a blue selection rectangle with handles, like in a slide editor. It appears in the first minute and never leaves:

- it selects the dull title and deletes it;
- it drags the chart's line off the slide;
- it frames a chain link, which is then deleted;
- it marks the next poster before each dive;
- it nudges the first domino;
- it lands on two calendar dates;
- it selects the Earth, then the Sun;
- it fails to land on a distant star;
- it becomes the divider between two futures;
- it marks "you are here";
- it finally selects the whole last slide.

It gives the deck a hand, makes changes feel caused rather than animated, and ties thirteen very different pictures together. The 16:9 rectangle recurs too: as pages, tiles, factory roofs, a target, cards.

**Pick one motif for your deck and let it do the work.**

A different kind of motif, from the twelve-scene film in [13](13-lessons-from-a-12-scene-film.md): a **running instrument**. A scale readout and location label stay on screen for the whole film, from 20 m on a beach to 3 nm inside a transistor, interpolated in log space, with a ruler that slides as the scale changes. When the talk's argument is a quantity (scale, time, money, speed), a gauge that measures it can be the through-line.

## Pacing

- Two to four beats per scene. More, and the scene should be two scenes.
- The picture moves first; the headline lands 1–3 s into the beat; it leaves in the first second of the next.
- One long ride per act (10–14 s), always the payoff.
- First beats start 1–3 s late so the transition can finish.
- Last beats hold longest.
- One hands-off set piece near the start (the note says: do not touch anything; let the deck work).

## Text on screen

- One headline, ten words or fewer. Numbers first.
- At most one small source line.
- Hedges are on screen: "illustration", a named source and date, "one modelled run".
- The disclaimer is an object in the scene (a paper label), not a footnote.

## Speaker notes

- Plain spoken sentences, second person, short.
- Concrete numbers with dated sources.
- Honest ranges and caveats stated outright.
- Stage directions in parentheses: say it flatly; wait; read the bullets slowly, then stop talking; let the counter run while you talk.
- A sentence decoding each visual metaphor.

## One source of truth for facts

Put every number the deck states in one object. The headline, the geometry and the speaker note all read from it, and a boot-time check fails if they disagree. When a figure is updated, the bar, the label and the sentence change together. (The film in [12](12-lessons-from-a-code-film.md) does this literally: the code typed on screen is executed, and the picture may read only the values it produces.)

## Open and close on the same image

The reference deck returns to its white template. The film opens with a cursor that becomes the sun and ends with everything collapsing to a point that is the cursor. Decide the rhyme before building anything.

## Small jokes that reward attention

- The page number is itself exponential: 1, 2, 3, then Fibonacci numbers, then powers of ten, then ∞, then 1.
- The dull chart's x-axis runs to 2070.
- The recap scene shows today's real date.

## Sound follows story

Each scene has its own bed and each transition its own cue, but all in one tuning. Sounds fire on events the audience can see. Quiet overall.

## Rules to carry to any deck

1. Write the talk as beats first: one sentence per click, before any pixels.
2. For each scene, find the object whose geometry is the claim.
3. Choose one motif and one colour for it.
4. Decide what each scene hands to the next.
5. Keep text to a headline and a source.
6. Put the caveats on screen.
7. Plan one hands-off moment and one false ending or callback.
8. End on something simple.
